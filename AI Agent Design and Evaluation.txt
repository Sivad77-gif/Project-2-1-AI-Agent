TASK 1: SCENARIO SELECTION
---------------------------------------------------------------------
Selected scenario: PERSONAL WELLNESS AI

Agent name: "WellPath"
An AI agent that helps people track and improve their physical and
mental health through daily check-ins, habit tracking, and
personalized, explainable wellness suggestions.


---------------------------------------------------------------------
TASK 2: DEFINE THE AI AGENT
---------------------------------------------------------------------
1. What data would this AI need to collect?
   - Daily mood check-ins (1-5 scale plus optional journal notes)
   - Sleep duration and quality (self-reported or from a wearable)
   - Physical activity (steps, workouts, active minutes)
   - Water intake and general eating habits (optional)
   - User-defined wellness goals (e.g., "sleep 7 hours," "walk daily")
   - Basic profile info the user chooses to share (age range,
     accessibility needs, preferred language)
   - Consent settings: what the user allows to be stored or shared
   All sensitive data is opt-in, encrypted, and deletable by the user.

2. How would it interact with users?
   - Through a mobile/web app with a conversational chat interface
   - Short daily check-in prompts (text or voice input)
   - Gentle, optional reminders (user sets frequency and quiet hours)
   - A dashboard showing trends in mood, sleep, and activity
   - Every suggestion includes a "Why am I seeing this?" explanation
   - Accessible design: screen-reader support, adjustable text size,
     multiple languages, and plain-language wording

3. What decisions would it make?
   - Which wellness suggestion to show (e.g., stretch, hydrate, rest,
     short breathing exercise) based on the check-in and goals
   - When to send a reminder, within the user's chosen schedule
   - Whether a check-in shows possible crisis warning signs; if so,
     it immediately surfaces crisis resources (e.g., 988 Suicide &
     Crisis Lifeline) and offers a connection to a human
   - How to adjust future suggestions based on user feedback
   It does NOT diagnose conditions or replace a medical professional.

4. What is its main goal?
   To help people build sustainable, healthy habits and better
   understand their own physical and mental well-being, while
   respecting their privacy, autonomy, and individual circumstances.

5. How could it be misused, even if unintentionally?
   - Health data could be sold to or shared with advertisers,
     insurers, or employers, leading to discrimination
   - Users might treat suggestions as medical advice and delay
     seeing a real doctor or therapist
   - Streaks, badges, and notifications could create compulsive use
     or guilt instead of healthy habits
   - Calorie/weight goals could reinforce disordered eating
   - Recommendations trained mostly on one population (e.g., young,
     able-bodied, affluent users) might not fit others
   - Employers could pressure staff to use it as a monitoring tool

Positive and ethical tasks (summary):
  WellPath generates personalized, explainable wellness suggestions;
  detects crisis signs and connects users to human help; recommends
  rest when needed; shows users their own trends; and adapts to
  different abilities, cultures, languages, and budgets.

Potentially unethical tasks (summary):
  1. Selling or sharing health data with advertisers, insurers, or
     employers (privacy violation, discrimination risk).
  2. Manipulative engagement: guilt-based streaks, excessive
     notifications, and upselling products (manipulation, unhealthy
     habits, profit over user well-being).
  3. (Additional) Using a "black box" model to label users as
     "high risk" or "unmotivated" without explanation, trained on
     data mostly from young, affluent, able-bodied users (bias and
     lack of transparency).

TASK 5: ETHICAL AI PRINCIPLES EVALUATION (Table 2-13)
---------------------------------------------------------------------

PRINCIPLE 1: EQUITY OVER EFFICIENCY
Questions: Does it prioritize fairness over profit? Is it regularly
audited for bias? Does it risk benefiting some while harming others?

Does WellPath uphold this principle? PARTIALLY - YES with safeguards.
  - The ethical design prioritizes user well-being over engagement
    and profit: no selling of data, no manipulative streaks, and core
    features (check-ins, crisis support, suggestions) are free.
  - The recommendation model is audited quarterly for bias by
    comparing suggestion quality and user outcomes across age,
    gender, race/ethnicity, disability, income, and language groups.
  - Risk: if the two unethical features (data selling, manipulative
    engagement) were enabled, profit would clearly win over fairness,
    and lower-income users could be harmed by insurance or employer
    discrimination. These features must be removed, not just limited.

PRINCIPLE 2: TRANSPARENCY AND EXPLAINABILITY
Questions: Can users understand how it makes decisions? Are its
actions traceable? Are data practices disclosed clearly?

Does WellPath uphold this principle? YES.
  - Every suggestion includes a "Why am I seeing this?" explanation
    (e.g., "You reported 5 hours of sleep and high stress, so we
    suggested a short rest break").
  - Each recommendation and safety alert is logged so the user (and
    auditors) can trace what data led to which action.
  - A short, plain-language privacy notice explains what is
    collected, why, and who can see it - shown before any data is
    stored, with easy "download my data" and "delete my data" options.
  - Risk: the "black box" risk-labeling feature would violate this
    principle; any user classification must be visible and
    explainable to the user.

PRINCIPLE 3: INCLUSIVE DESIGN PRACTICES
Questions: Whose perspectives were considered? Could it
unintentionally exclude or harm? Is it usable across diverse
populations?

Does WellPath uphold this principle? MOSTLY - YES, with ongoing work.
  - Design includes input from doctors, mental health professionals,
    disability advocates, older adults, low-income users, and
    people from different cultural and language backgrounds.
  - Supports screen readers, voice input, large text, multiple
    languages, and seated/low-mobility exercise options.
  - Avoids weight-loss and calorie pressure by default to reduce harm
    to people with a history of eating disorders.
  - Risk: users without smartphones, wearables, or reliable internet
    may be excluded. Mitigation: a low-data web version and
    SMS-based check-ins. A "neutral" standard like "10,000 steps a
    day" may not fit everyone, so goals are user-defined.

PRINCIPLE 4: HISTORICAL AND SOCIAL AWARENESS
Questions: Is training data free from legacy bias? Could it impact
society or behavior long term? Are embedded assumptions questioned?

Does WellPath uphold this principle? PARTIALLY - requires vigilance.
  - Historical health data has well-known biases (e.g., BMI standards
    and medical research that underrepresented women and people of
    color). WellPath does not use BMI as a success measure and
    reviews training data for representation gaps.
  - Embedded assumptions (that "healthy" looks the same for everyone,
    that more activity is always better) are questioned by an
    advisory board that reviews content annually.
  - Long-term impact: positive if it builds self-awareness and
    healthy habits; negative if it creates dependence on an app,
    normalizes constant self-tracking, or lets employers and insurers
    use wellness data against people. WellPath encourages real-world
    human support and never shares data without explicit consent.
  - Training data can never be completely free of legacy bias, so
    continuous monitoring, user feedback, and correction are required.


---------------------------------------------------------------------
CONCLUSION: GUIDE FOR FAIR, TRANSPARENT, INCLUSIVE, AND SOCIALLY
AWARE AI
---------------------------------------------------------------------
1. Fairness:      Put user well-being ahead of profit; audit for bias
                  regularly across demographic groups.
2. Transparency:  Explain every decision in plain language; keep
                  traceable logs; disclose data practices up front.
3. Inclusion:     Design with diverse users and experts; support
                  accessibility, languages, and low-resource access.
4. Awareness:     Question legacy data and "neutral" standards;
                  consider long-term effects on individuals and society.
5. Privacy:       Collect minimal data, require opt-in consent, never
                  sell health data, and let users delete everything.
6. Human safety:  Never replace professionals; escalate crises to
                  human help immediately.

---------------------------------------------------------------------
 DISCUSSION QUESTIONS
---------------------------------------------------------------------
1. After designing and evaluating your AI agent, what is the most significant ethical challenge you identified?

For me, the biggest challenge was privacy. WellPath only works well if people share really personal things like their mood, sleep, and stress levels. That same information could hurt someone if it got into the wrong hands. If a company sold it to an insurer or an employer, a person could end up paying more or even being treated differently at work just because they had a rough month. It surprised me how easily a helpful feature could turn into something harmful.

2. How might you mitigate this challenge in a real-world application?

I would make everything opt-in, so users choose what they share, and I'd only collect what the app actually needs. The data would be encrypted, and users could download or delete all of it whenever they want. I'd also have a clear rule that health data is never sold or shared without the person's permission. Finally, I'd write the privacy policy in plain language so people actually understand it instead of just clicking "agree."

3. What was the most challenging part of creating the design for this agent?

Honestly, the hardest part was balancing helpfulness with not being pushy. Features like streaks and reminders can motivate people, but they can also make someone feel guilty or stressed, which defeats the whole point of a wellness app. Figuring out what to do when someone shows signs of a crisis was also tough. I had to accept that the AI shouldn't try to handle that on its own and should connect the person to a real human instead.

4. What new insights did you gain about AI ethics and design?

I learned that ethics has to be part of the design from the start, not something you fix later. Before this project, I thought bias mostly came from bad intentions. Now I see it can come from something as simple as training data that doesn't include everyone, or a "normal" standard that doesn't fit every person. I also realized how important it is for users to understand why the AI suggests something. If people can't see how it makes decisions, it's hard for them to trust it.
